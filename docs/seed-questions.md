# Seed Questions

Seven for Kenny to ask Dean if the Q&A opens quiet. Runs 0:54–1:00, after Poll 2.

**Every one is a product management problem, and every one is anchored in something the room just watched.** Not "how do I adopt GitHub" — that is a tool question nobody registered for. Not "how do I deal with a new VP" — that is a career question with no connection to the last fifty minutes. These are the questions a product manager asks *because* they saw Kenny build a repo, point Claude at four files, run a sweep, and open a pull request.

**Kenny asks them from where he sat** — he did all of it an hour ago, so he can point at the screen. "When I did that thing with the four files…"

**Use them in order and stop as soon as real questions arrive.** One seed to warm the room is normal. Five in a row is a monologue.

**Every answer carries an honest limit.** Nothing here is solved by a folder, and saying so is what makes the rest believable.

---

## 1. "When I pointed Claude at those four files it gave a useful answer. I've tried that before and gotten generic mush. What was different?"

**Anchored in:** Play 1, Rung 2 — Claude reading Kenny's repo.

**The real problem:** everyone in the room has tried AI for product work and gotten strategy that could be about any company. They have concluded they are bad at prompting.

**The answer's spine:**
- It was not the prompt. It was that the answers already existed in a form it could read.
- A better prompt is still you typing your product into a box every morning, from memory, slightly differently each time.
- Four stub files beat a long prompt because they persist, they get corrected, and the corrections stay corrected.
- **The limit:** it will not decide anything for you. It gets much better at drafting and much better at arguing with you. It still does not know which customer matters most.

---

## 2. "Everything Kenny wrote down was true this morning. My strategy changes constantly. How does this not become a museum of things that used to be true?"

**Anchored in:** Play 1 — the file history and the decision record.

**The real problem:** the room has been burned by documentation. They have all maintained a wiki that quietly went stale and then lied to people for a year.

**The answer's spine:**
- A wiki goes stale because it pretends to be current. A record with dates on it does not pretend anything.
- "We believed this in August" stays true forever. That is the difference between a record and a status page.
- The superseded decision is not clutter. It is the thing that tells your successor the market moved, rather than that you were careless.
- **The limit:** it will go stale anyway if nothing writes to it. The fix is that updating happens as a side effect of working, not as a monthly cleanup nobody schedules.

---

## 3. "Kenny added Dean to his repo. Most of my stakeholders will never open GitHub. Does any of this help me with them?"

**Anchored in:** Play 2 — the collaborator invite, and the access caveat named in Play 5.

**The real problem:** the PM's hardest audience is executives and sales, and none of them are going to create an account.

**The answer's spine:**
- Be honest first: they will not go there, and you should not try to make them.
- What changes is what you bring to them. You stop arriving with recollection and start arriving with what you knew, when, and what you traded away.
- The record is for you and the people doing the work. The output is what stakeholders see, and it gets better because the input stopped being your memory.
- **The limit:** this is genuinely a gap. GitHub does not give you open participation, and anyone who tells you a stakeholder portal falls out of this is selling something.

---

## 4. "That sweep produced a lot, fast. How do I know which of it to trust, and how often do I re-run it?"

**Anchored in:** Play 3 — the market intelligence sweep.

**The real problem:** volume is not confidence, and the room has been handed impressive-looking competitive decks that fell apart under a single question.

**The answer's spine:**
- Read the section that says what it will not claim, first. That section is why the rest is trustworthy.
- Every claim carries a source and a date, so the question stops being "do I believe this" and becomes "is this still true."
- Six sources are often two disciplines wearing different hats. Independence is what stacks confidence, not volume.
- Re-run it when a decision depends on it, not on a calendar. The second run is a diff, not a rebuild.
- **The limit:** it collects what is public. It cannot tell you why you lost a deal. Only the person who lost it can.

---

## 5. "Dean reviewed Kenny's pull request. I'm the only product manager on my team. Who's my Dean?"

**Anchored in:** Play 5 — the review and merge.

**The real problem:** solo PMs have no second pair of eyes, and they know their blind spots are invisible to them by definition.

**The answer's spine:**
- The reviewer does not have to be another PM. An engineer, a designer, or a support lead reading your reasoning will catch different things and usually better ones.
- Reviewing a proposal is a smaller ask than attending a meeting. People decline meetings and answer questions.
- And when there is genuinely nobody: the record still makes you the reviewer, six weeks later, which is not nothing. Most of what you would have caught, you catch by rereading.
- **The limit:** self-review is not peer review. If you are alone, the honest move is to widen the circle, not to feel covered.

---

## 6. "We saw a place to put things and explore. If I drop a throwaway prototype in there, how do I stop someone finding it and thinking it's the roadmap?"

**Anchored in:** Play 5 — the space to experiment.

**The real problem:** prototyping got cheap, and the room has been burned by it. An executive sees a working mockup and treats it as a commitment. Now the PM is negotiating a date for something built to be discarded.

**The answer's spine:**
- The fear is correct and it is not a reason to stop. Cheap prototypes are the best thing to happen to discovery in a decade.
- A throwaway with no label is indistinguishable from a plan. Somebody found an artifact and supplied their own context, because none came with it.
- Give it the assumption it was testing, what happened, and whether it is dead. That turns a promise into evidence.
- The quieter failure: six months on nobody remembers which four you built or what any taught you, so someone builds one of them again.
- **The limit:** nothing stops an executive getting excited. It gives you something better than "that wasn't real" — what you learned, and why you stopped.

---

## 7. "Kenny cloned your skills library and ran one. If my whole team had that, would we actually do the work the same way?"

**Anchored in:** Play 3, and the contribution in Play 5.

**The real problem:** method does not stick. Four people attend the same training, return to different teams, and quality ends up depending on who happened to run the work.

**The answer's spine:**
- Training in a binder competes with how people already work and loses. Not from disagreement — remembering it is extra effort under deadline.
- A method that lives where the work happens does not have to be remembered. It is what is sitting there when you start.
- The version that survives is the one the team can improve. When somebody hits a case it handles badly and can fix it for everyone, they stop treating it as someone else's process. That is why the fifth play was a contribution rather than a download.
- **The limit:** this will not make anyone use a method they do not believe in, and a mediocre method applied consistently is worse than inconsistency. Buy consistency only once the method is good.
