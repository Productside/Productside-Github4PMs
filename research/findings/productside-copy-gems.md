## This resolves the "is it the same webinar" question

The marketing template confirms: **Wednesday, September 2, 2026, 1PM ET / 10AM
PT**, speaker Dean Peters, moderator Kenny Kranseler, recommended title **"How
to Set Up a GitHub for Your Product Team."** That's the same date named as
"the one deadline" in `productside-launchkit`'s `HANDOFF.md`, and the title is
close enough to that repo's draft webinar ("Setting Up a GitHub Presence for a
Company That Doesn't Build Software") that these are almost certainly the same
event, not two different ones.

Best working theory: **one webinar**, structured around the Five Plays
(Context, Communications, Experimentation, Governance, Community), where the
**Governance play absorbs the launchkit's category-trap / licensing / secrets
/ Content Guard material** rather than that material getting its own separate
nine-module show. That would explain why the launchkit draft and the Five
Plays outline overlap on CODEOWNERS, branch protection, and guardrail
automation — they're not competing drafts, they're the same governance
territory viewed from two different starting points (Productside's own
internal setup vs. general PM audience). Worth a two-minute confirmation from
Dean rather than assuming.

**Separately, and worth not conflating with the above:** the internal training
doc proposes a *different, smaller* session — a non-technical Lunch & Learn for
Productside's own staff, suggested for the week of July 13/16, 2026, well
before the September webinar. That's an internal audience (Productside
employees who "will not need to manage GitHub every day") vs. the webinar's
external audience (Product Managers at other companies). Same source material,
two different talks. Keep them as two builds, not one.

## Copy fuel worth lifting directly

**The disarming line to open with**, already in Dean's voice and doing exactly
what the audience guardrail in the project brief asks for (plain language,
nobody needs to become a developer):

> "Not everything belongs there. Not everything should be public. And nobody
> is being asked to become a developer."

**The vivid one-line description of what GitHub actually is**, better than
anything pulled from the AI research docs because it's concrete and stacks
four familiar categories instead of one abstract metaphor:

> "Think of it as part digital vault, part publishing platform, part
> collaboration space, and part institutional memory."

**The "practice what we teach" hook** — this is the sharpest, most authentic
line in the whole document, and it's a natural cold open or closing beat for a
Product Manager audience specifically, since it's making exactly the argument
the webinar's whole premise depends on:

> "We teach clients not to operate as 'One Team' in name while everyone works
> in separate silos, folders, sessions, and projects. We should not work that
> way either."

**The two-sentence business case**, cleaner than the five-play framing for an
audience that hasn't bought in yet — could work as the actual opening frame,
with the five plays as the "here's how" that follows it:

> "GitHub matters to Productside in two ways: it helps us manage our own work
> better, and it helps us show the market how we work."

**The objection-and-answer pattern**, worth reusing as a structural device for
an entire module, not just the one place it appears here:

> "The obvious concern is: 'Could we accidentally expose something sensitive?'
> Yes, if we were sloppy. Which is why we are not being sloppy."

That's a genuinely reusable rhetorical shape: name the fear the audience is
already having, concede it plainly, then show the guardrail that answers it.
Better than either burying the risk or over-reassuring past it.

**The punchier version of "public is a publishing decision"**, worth swapping
in for the launchkit's slightly more formal phrasing:

> "Start private. Make it public only when we mean to. Turning something
> public should feel like a small launch, not a casual settings change."

## A governance rule not yet in the launchkit — worth reconciling

The training doc's "Six Non-Negotiables" match the launchkit's Constitution
almost exactly, **except this version has a sixth rule the launchkit docs
gathered so far don't contain**:

> "GitHub Copilot: Productside standardizes on Claude as our company AI tool.
> GitHub Copilot is a separate product from Copilot. It is a paid feature
> that we do not use, and it should not be enabled or used on any Productside
> repository... One AI stack keeps our data, review process, and standards
> consistent. No Copilot. No exceptions."

Whether or not the actual `CONSTITUTION.md` gets updated with this (Dean's
call, flagged below), the underlying rule generalizes well and is a genuinely
new governance beat none of the other four research piles surfaced: **pick one
AI coding assistant for your org and standardize on it, rather than letting
different tools proliferate across repos with different data-handling and
review implications.** That's a sharper, more current governance point than
anything in the web research or the launchkit's existing chapters — worth its
own beat in the Governance play, especially for an AI Product Management
audience that's actively choosing between Copilot, Cursor, and Claude Code
right now.

## Appendix 1 as raw benefit-statement copy

The training doc's two long lists ("Internal Advantages," "External
Advantages") are the single richest vein of ready-made one-liners in
anything gathered across all four research piles, precisely because Dean
already wrote them in exactly the register a Productside audience expects.
Worth treating as a copy bank to pull from for slide bullets, a "why bother"
section, registration-page "What You Will Learn" bullets, or social post
copy — rather than rewriting equivalents from scratch. A sample, but the full
lists in the source file are worth a direct read before drafting any of those
assets:

- "Easy rollback — Restore earlier versions when needed."
- "Less tribal memory — Knowledge survives beyond individuals."
- "Draft-to-official path — Turn rough work into trusted assets."
- "Visible credibility — Show that Productside practices what it teaches."
- "Public proof — Give prospects something real to inspect."
- "Sales enablement — Give sales concrete proof points."
- "Recruiting signal — Show how Productside works."
- "Brand authority — Look like a contributor, not just a vendor."

Each list closes with an "In plain English" summary line, which is worth
keeping as a recurring device throughout the webinar itself, not just this
internal doc — it's consistent with the project brief's own voice guardrails
and gives the show a repeatable rhythm audiences can start predicting (in a
good way).

## What the marketing-copy template tells us we'll eventually need

Not gems, but useful to have mapped out now rather than discovering it late.
Once the outline and content are locked, this project will need:

- a 75–150 word abstract, 3–5 key takeaways, a "who should attend" blurb, and
  speaker bios (Dean Peters + Kenny Kranseler as moderator)
- LinkedIn Live copy and registration-page copy (title, subtitle, ~75-word
  description, "What You Will Learn" bullets)
- four social posts at different lead times (3 weeks out, 2 weeks out, 1 week
  out as a poll, plus a live-reminder and an on-demand post), each with
  platform-specific length limits already documented in the template (LinkedIn
  3,000 characters, X 280 characters, HubSpot best practice under 250 for body
  text)
- five email variants (announcement, newsletter blurb, reminder, 1-hour
  reminder, on-demand follow-up), each with its HubSpot list targeting already
  specified
- a YouTube description

None of this blocks outline or content work, but it's worth keeping in the
task list for whenever this project moves into promotion — the shell is
already built, it just needs the copy once there's something to describe.
